Experiential Immanence

IAppearing

§1Appearing as the point of departure

The inquiry begins with whatever presents itself. This is provisionally called . Perceiving, feeling, remembering, imagining, estimating, judging, and doubting are all admitted for consideration, and admitting them requires no assent to what they seem to disclose. This beginning is a methodological undertaking. It is not a proof that appearing exists as an ultimate reality. A denial of appearing can itself be treated as an appearance.[4] This is not taken to establish an indubitable foundation.

§2Experience and the scope of inquiry

is used here as a name for appearing. Using it posits no substance, no container for representations, and no experiencer distinct from what appears.[3][7][9] The inquiry attends to what appears and to how it appears. The how is termed its .[6] The term presupposes neither certainty nor a recipient. Does givenness require a recipient? That remains suspended. So do three further questions: whether experience exhausts reality, whether anything exists independently of it, and whether that distinction is ultimately adequate.

§3Phenomenal parity

No mode of appearing is excluded merely because it is doubtful, conceptual, imagined, or apparently mistaken. This is . It concerns admission to inquiry. It does not declare appearances identical, and it does not declare their claims equally credible. Perception may appear compelling, recollection uncertain, and imagination freely variable. These differences can be described, and they can inform estimates. They are not thereby accepted as guarantees of truth. Interpretation and are admitted in the same way. The inquiry requires no foundation of uninterpreted sensation.[1][11]

IISuspension

§4Epoché

The governing practice is . It is the withholding of from claims presented as true, as known, or as settled accounts of reality. To suspend a claim is to affirm neither the claim nor its negation.[10] Suspension need not eliminate belief or conviction as they are felt. It need not eliminate the felt inevitability of assent either. Such feelings can themselves be treated as appearances. A claim can still guide deliberation and action. It does so as an , without being held as true or known outright.[2][8] Suspension applies to claims of fallible knowledge as much as to claims of certainty. “I know this, though I may be wrong” still holds something as known. Is suspension complete, and is it consistently maintained? That too is open to examination.

§5Description and commitment

A description attempts to articulate what appears and how it appears. An ontological or epistemic commitment, by contrast, assents to a claim about what exists or what is known. The distinction lies in how a formulation is used. It does not divide reality into two separate regions. “There seems to be a cup” can function as a provisional description. It is not thereby an infallible report. Descriptions remain selective, interpretive, and revisable. Neither their wording nor their apparent fidelity exempts them from suspension.

IIIHow things appear

§6Intentionality and horizon

Appearing may have the character of being of something, or about something. Where it does, this directedness is termed . What appears is considered in the manner in which it appears: as perceived, remembered, imagined, anticipated, or otherwise presented. Take a cup. Its visible side may be seen while its reverse is implicit, without being seen. Such implicit aspects, together with the further appearances that are anticipated, constitute a .[6] Neither directedness nor horizon is taken to settle whether an independent object exists. And the inquiry does not require every appearance to have an object.

§7Manifestation and reflection

An experience can be described as occurring without any separate act of observing it. is a provisional name for this character. It does not establish that every experience knows itself or contains an inner witness.[5][12][13] Reflection occurs where experience itself becomes what is attended to. Reflection is not taken to supply guaranteed access to an unchanged original, since attending, recollecting, and describing may alter what is presented afterwards. Neither reflection nor apparent immediacy is taken to confer infallibility.

§8Perspective and subject

Where a appears, it is described without concluding that a underlies it.[13][14] Bodily orientation, a sense of ownership, and an explicit thought of “I” need not be treated as one and the same appearance. How they relate remains a matter for inquiry. The presence of an “I”-thought is not taken to establish an independently existing experiencer. Its absence is not taken to establish experience without a subject. A substantial self, a subjectless reality, and other interpretations receive no assent. None of them is a conclusion of the system. Another person’s face, voice, and movements may appear, and they may appear expressive of experience. Whether that person has experience of their own remains suspended. does not depend on settling it. On an estimate formed from how they appear, one can go on treating them as someone.

§9Co-givenness and field

Where appearances present themselves together, their can be described. names this apparent togetherness and the differences within it. The name posits no container, no unifying entity, and no necessary unity of all experience. Nearness, distance, depth, separation, and difference are considered as they appear. Describing them is not taken to settle whether anything exists independently, or how it would be related. Fragmentation, discontinuity, and uncertainty about what belongs together are equally admissible.

§10Temporality and recollection

Succession, duration, persistence, anticipation, and recollection are each considered in their own mode of appearing. A recollection is admitted as recollection, without assent to the past it presents. An anticipation is admitted without assent to the future it projects. Apparent recurrence is not taken to establish a necessary law, though it may inform an estimate. Historical claims remain suspended, whether they are offered as true or as known. This includes claims about the inquiry’s own earlier stages. The suspension does not establish that such claims are false. Nor does it demonstrate that historical knowledge is impossible.

IVLiving with it

§11Reasoning, evidence, and explanation

Descriptions, arguments, evidence, and explanations can all be examined and provisionally employed. Their differences are kept. An argument offers reasons. Evidence bears on a claim. An explanation proposes grounds, causes, mechanisms, or conditions. That they occur as appearances is not, by itself, taken to validate or invalidate them. Further inquiry may change the estimates of which account is better supported, and suspension does not end because of it. Experiential Immanence does not claim to refute competing accounts of reality. Nor does it claim privileged access to truth for itself. Its concepts, inferences, and formulations remain answerable to criticism and revision.

§12Reflexivity and practice

Suspension applies to every claim to truth or knowledge. That includes the claims made on behalf of this system, which are held only as estimates. “Only experience exists,” “nothing can be known,” and “suspension is the uniquely correct method” receive no exemption. Practical reliance does not end suspension. What recollections and perceptions present may inform estimates that guide action, without being held as true or known outright. Remembered success may raise the estimate placed on memory. But it supplies no independent guarantee of memory’s reliability. Such reliance remains revisable in light of further appearances. The practice invites one to examine how an anticipation becomes a settled future, how an interpretation comes to be held as fact, how an evaluation becomes a settled verdict, or how a sense of self becomes an asserted entity. Pain, distress, conviction, and resistance need not disappear, and they need not lose their force. The system promises neither relief nor liberation. It supplies no complete rule for reasonable or ethical action.

§13Practical reliance and holding as settled

In outward conduct, practical reliance need not differ from ordinary belief. The difference lies in how a claim is held: as an estimate that guides action, not as true or known outright, and not as a settled account of reality. When what appears later goes against an estimate, the estimate is revised. It is not refuted, since it did not hold the claim as true.[2][10]

Take the claim that the road continues after the bend. It can be held as true: “it continues”, or, fallibly, “it continues, though I could be wrong”. Suspension withholds this. It can be left suspended: “does it continue?”, with neither the claim nor its negation affirmed. Or it can be held as an estimate formed from the road seen so far: “it probably continues”. This estimate guides action. If the road ends after the bend, a claim held as true is refuted, while the estimate is only revised.

Suspension does not require a contrary action. It does not require conviction to disappear, or constant verbal reminders of uncertainty. What it invites is a change in how things are taken. What appears compelling may keep its force while being held only as an estimate, not as true or known, not even fallibly. Such a change is a possibility of the practice. It is not a necessary result, and it is not a test of the practice’s success. The practice is undertaken for the sake of such a change. That it produces one is not claimed.
