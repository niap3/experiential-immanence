Experiential Immanence

  1. Whatever is encountered is encountered in . Perceiving, feeling, remembering, imagining, judging and doubting are admitted in the manners in which they occur. Even the attempt to contest appearing is encountered as an experience; this does not establish what exists independently of experience.
  2. designates appearing in its lived character. It names neither a substance nor a receptacle in which representations are contained. The domain of the present inquiry is what is given, together with its . No claim concerning the exhaustive constitution of reality follows from this restriction.
  3. Differences between experiential modes are retained without assigning one mode foundational privilege over the others. This concerns their admissibility as experience. A perception, a recollection and an imagined possibility need not have equal determinacy or evidential force. Interpretation and belong to experience; description does not presuppose an uninterpreted sensory stratum beneath them.
  4. The method is : is suspended wherever a commitment exceeds what is given. Suspension concerns endorsement, not the elimination of an experiential content. A belief, its meaning and its felt certainty remain admissible as experienced even when the assertion it expresses is left undecided.
  5. A determination is insofar as it articulates appearing as given. A exceeds that articulation. The distinction is between an experienced claim and what the claim warrants us in asserting; it is not a partition between two regions of experience. Withholding assent need not leave the subsequent course or affective character of experience unchanged.
  6. Where experience presents itself as of or about something, this is admitted. What is perceived, remembered or anticipated must be described according to its respective mode of givenness. The visible aspect of a cup and the of its unseen reverse are distinguishable within experience; anticipation of the reverse does not amount to its present visual perception. Neither directedness nor anticipation alone establishes an independently existing referent.
  7. Experience is described here as having : its being lived does not require a second act observing it. Reflection is a further mode of experience in which experience becomes thematic. Neither lived presence nor reflective attention entails exhaustive or infallible knowledge of what is experienced.
  8. The of experience is distinguished from a . The visible body, felt posture, sense of agency, self-reference and autobiographical recollection are admitted in their respective modes. Their manifestation does not by itself establish a bearer separate from experience. Such a bearer is neither affirmed nor denied by this method; first-personality is not abolished by suspending that commitment.
  9. Where togetherness is experienced, is admitted. “” names this articulation without introducing a container or an additional unifying entity. Experienced depth, distance, proximity and differentiation are immanent as experienced. Their appearance does not alone settle relations among independently existing things.
  10. includes experienced succession and duration, remembering as remembering and anticipation as anticipation. Apparent recurrence is admitted without being elevated into necessary law. The occurrence of a recollection does not by itself establish its ; this does not declare historical inquiry impossible.
  11. Description articulates what appears and how it appears. Explanation introduces grounds, causes or conditions whose warrant requires further argument or evidence. Experiential Immanence neither supplies nor prohibits such explanations. Its own concepts and formulations remain interpretations within experience and acquire no exemption from its method.
  12. The practical exercise consists in distinguishing what is experienced from what is additionally endorsed. It can be directed against , including the treatment of an anticipated future or an inferred self as conclusively established. Pain and distress are admitted without diminution. Any alleviation consequent upon suspension remains contingent; it is not deduced from the system.

Terminological determinations
appearing
Manifestation in any experiential mode. The term is not restricted to visual appearance and does not imply an opposition between illusion and reality.
experience
Appearing considered in its lived character. This specifies the domain of description, not the totality of independently existing entities.
mode of givenness
The manner in which something is experienced: for example, as perceived, remembered, imagined, anticipated or judged. These distinctions concern how something appears, not strata of reality.
phenomenal parity
Equal admissibility for description qua experience. This is a stipulated methodological principle, not a claim of equal vividness, justification, truth or causal independence.
epoché
Suspension of assent to an existential or explanatory commitment that exceeds what is given. The thought, its meaning and its felt conviction remain describable. This use does not adopt the entirety of Husserl’s transcendental phenomenology.
assent
Endorsement of a proposition as true. Entertaining, understanding or experiencing conviction about a proposition is distinguishable from the warrant for endorsing it.
immanent
Belonging to appearing as given, including its meaningful, affective, bodily and temporal articulations. This does not mean spatially inside a mind.
transcendent commitment
An assertion that exceeds what is given in the experience under description. Its experiential occurrence as a thought is distinguished from the existence or truth it asserts.
intentionality
The of-or-about character of experience where that character appears. Intentional directedness does not by itself establish the independent existence of its object, and is not synonymous with deliberate intention.
horizon
What is implicated or anticipated without being presented in the same manner as the currently perceived aspect. An anticipated reverse side is given as anticipated, not as currently seen.
pre-reflective self-manifestation
The lived presence of experience prior to making it an object of reflection. Adopted here as a phenomenological characterization, not as infallible self-knowledge or proof of a separate observer.
first-personal character
The perspectival, lived character of experience. It is distinguished here from an explicit thought of oneself and from the metaphysical claim of a separate owner.
substantial subject
A bearer posited as existing in its own right behind or apart from experiential manifestation. This narrower term avoids treating every sense of subject or self as a separable soul.
co-givenness
The experienced togetherness of experiential aspects, insofar as that togetherness is manifest. It does not establish that every experience belongs to one universal field.
phenomenal field
An expression for experienced co-givenness and differentiation. It is neither an independently existing container nor necessarily a visual or spatial expanse.
phenomenal temporality
Experienced succession, duration, recollection and anticipation. Their admission does not settle the metaphysical nature of time.
veridicality
Accuracy or truthfulness of an experience in relation to what it presents. An experience’s occurrence alone does not establish its veridicality.
underdetermination
Insufficiency of a specified evidential basis to decide between claims. Here the basis is appearing taken solely as appearing, not all conceivable argument or evidence.
reification
Treating an abstraction, interpretation or experienced structure as an independently existing thing without sufficient warrant.
proprioception
The experienced position and movement of one’s body. Distinct from seeing the body or feeling contact at its surface.
affectivity
The felt character of experience, including moods and emotions. It need not be a discrete object or an evenly distributed tone.
conceptual apprehension
Experiencing something as meaningful or as something of a kind. It need not occur as a separate verbal label added to perception.

Terminological context: Intentionality; Phenomenological approaches to self-consciousness; Phenomenology. These sources situate the vocabulary; they do not establish every proposition of this system.
