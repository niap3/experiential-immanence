Experiential Immanence

IAppearing

  1. Appearing as the point of departure

    The inquiry begins with whatever presents itself, provisionally termed . Perceiving, feeling, remembering, imagining, estimating, judging, and doubting are admitted for consideration without requiring to what they seem to disclose. This beginning is a methodological undertaking, not a proof that appearing exists as an ultimate reality. A denial of appearing may itself be considered as an appearance; this is not taken to establish an indubitable foundation.

  2. Experience and the scope of inquiry

    is used here as a name for appearing. Its use posits neither a substance, a container for representations, nor an experiencer distinct from what appears. The inquiry attends to what appears and how it appears, termed its . The term is used without presupposing certainty or a recipient; whether givenness requires a recipient remains suspended. Whether experience exhausts reality, whether anything exists independently of it, and whether that distinction is ultimately adequate remain suspended.

  3. Phenomenal parity

    No mode of appearing is excluded merely because it is doubtful, conceptual, imagined, or apparently mistaken. This concerns admission to inquiry; it does not declare appearances identical or their claims equally credible. Perception may appear compelling, recollection uncertain, and imagination freely variable. These differences may be described, and may inform estimates, without being accepted as guarantees of truth. Interpretation and are likewise admitted; the inquiry requires no foundation of uninterpreted sensation.

IISuspension

  1. Epoché

    The governing practice is : withholding assent to claims presented as true, as known, or as settled accounts of reality. Suspension neither affirms a claim nor affirms its negation. It need not eliminate belief or conviction as they are felt, nor the felt inevitability of assent; these may themselves be considered as appearances. A claim may guide deliberation and action as an , without being held as true or known outright. Suspension applies to claims of fallible knowledge as well as claims of certainty. Whether suspension is complete or consistently maintained is itself open to examination.

  2. Description and commitment

    A description attempts to articulate what appears and how it appears. An ontological or epistemic commitment assents to a claim about what exists or what is known. The distinction concerns how a formulation is used, not two separate regions of reality. “There seems to be a cup” can function as a provisional description; it is not thereby an infallible report. Descriptions remain selective, interpretive, and revisable. Neither their wording nor their apparent fidelity exempts them from suspension.

IIIHow things appear

  1. Intentionality and horizon

    Where appearing has the character of being of or about something, this directedness is termed . What appears is considered in the manner in which it appears: as perceived, remembered, imagined, anticipated, or otherwise presented. A cup’s visible side may be seen while its reverse is implicit without being seen. Such implicit aspects, together with anticipated further appearances, constitute a . Neither directedness nor horizon is taken to settle whether an independent object exists. The inquiry does not require every appearance to have an object.

  2. Manifestation and reflection

    An experience may be described as occurring without a separate act of observing it. provisionally names this character; it does not establish that every experience knows itself or contains an inner witness. Reflection occurs where experience itself becomes a theme of attention. It is not taken to supply guaranteed access to an unchanged original: attending, recollecting, and describing may alter what is subsequently presented. Neither reflection nor apparent immediacy is taken to confer infallibility.

  3. Perspective and subject

    Where a appears, it is described without concluding that a underlies it. Bodily orientation, a sense of ownership, and an explicit thought of “I” need not be treated as the same appearance. Their relations remain matters for inquiry. The presence of an “I”-thought is not taken to establish an independently existing experiencer; its absence is not taken to establish experience without a subject. A substantial self, a subjectless reality, and other interpretations receive no assent; none is a conclusion of the system. Another person’s face, voice, and movements may appear, and may appear expressive of experience. Whether they have experience of their own remains suspended. does not depend on settling it: on an estimate formed from how they appear, one may go on treating them as someone.

  4. Co-givenness and field

    Where appearances present themselves together, their may be described. names this apparent togetherness, and the differences within it, without positing a container, a unifying entity, or a necessary unity of all experience. Nearness, distance, depth, separation, and difference are considered as they appear. Their description is not taken to settle whether anything exists independently, or how it would be related. Fragmentation, discontinuity, and uncertainty about what belongs together are equally admissible.

  5. Temporality and recollection

    Succession, duration, persistence, anticipation, and recollection are considered in their respective modes of appearing. A recollection is admitted as recollection without assent to the past it presents. An anticipation is admitted without assent to the future it projects. Apparent recurrence is not taken to establish a necessary law, though it may inform an estimate. Historical claims, including claims about the inquiry’s own earlier stages, remain suspended, whether offered as true or as known. This suspension neither establishes their falsity nor demonstrates that historical knowledge is impossible.

IVLiving with it

  1. Reasoning, evidence, and explanation

    Descriptions, arguments, evidence, and explanations may all be examined and provisionally employed. Their differences are retained: an argument offers reasons, evidence bears on a claim, and an explanation proposes grounds, causes, mechanisms, or conditions. Their occurring as appearances is not by itself taken to validate or invalidate them. Further inquiry may change estimates of which account is better supported without ending suspension. Experiential Immanence claims neither to refute competing accounts of reality nor to establish its own privileged access to truth. Its concepts, inferences, and formulations remain answerable to criticism and revision.

  2. Reflexivity and practice

    Suspension applies to every claim to truth or knowledge, including claims made on behalf of this system, which are held only as estimates. “Only experience exists,” “nothing can be known,” and “suspension is the uniquely correct method” receive no exemption. Practical reliance does not terminate suspension: what recollections and perceptions present may inform estimates that guide action, without being held as true or known outright. Remembered success may raise the estimate placed on memory, but supplies no independent guarantee of its reliability. Such reliance remains revisable in light of further appearances. The practice invites examination of how an anticipation becomes a settled future, an interpretation comes to be held as fact, an evaluation becomes a settled verdict, or a sense of self becomes an asserted entity. Pain, distress, conviction, and resistance need not disappear or lose their force. The system promises neither relief nor liberation, and supplies no complete rule for reasonable or ethical action.

  3. Practical reliance and holding as settled

    Practical reliance need not differ from ordinary belief in outward conduct. The distinction concerns how a claim is held: as an estimate that guides action, not as true or known outright, and not as a settled account of reality. When what later appears goes against an estimate, the estimate is revised rather than refuted, since it did not hold the claim as true. Suspension does not require a contrary action, the disappearance of conviction, or constant verbal reminders of uncertainty. It invites a change in how things are taken: what appears compelling may retain its force while being held only as an estimate, not as true or known, even fallibly. Such a change is a possibility of the practice, not a necessary result or a test of its success. The practice is undertaken for the sake of such a change; that it produces one is not claimed.
