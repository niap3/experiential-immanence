Experiential Immanence

  1. Whatever is encountered is encountered in . Perceiving, feeling, remembering, imagining, judging, and doubting are admitted as they occur. Even an attempt to deny appearing is experienced. None of this shows what exists independently of experience.
  2. means appearing as it is lived. It is neither a substance nor a container for representations. This inquiry concerns what is given and how it is given: its . The restriction does not imply that experience is the whole of reality.
  3. Modes of experience are kept distinct, and none is granted foundational privilege. This concerns their admissibility as experience, not their equality as evidence. A perception, a recollection, and an imagined possibility may differ in determinacy and evidential force. Interpretation and also belong to experience; description need not assume a layer of uninterpreted sensation beneath them.
  4. The method is : suspending whenever a commitment goes beyond what is given. Suspension withholds endorsement; it does not remove anything experienced. A belief, its meaning, and its felt certainty can still be described while the claim it expresses stays undecided.
  5. A determination is when it says what appears, as it appears. A says more than that. The distinction is between a claim as experienced and the truth of what it asserts, not between two regions of experience. Suspending assent may change how experience goes on and how it feels.
  6. Where experience presents itself as of or about something, this is admitted as part of what appears. What is perceived, remembered, imagined, or anticipated must be described in its own mode of givenness. The visible side of a cup is seen; its reverse belongs to the , anticipated as part of the cup without being seen. Neither directedness nor anticipation shows that its apparent object exists independently.
  7. Experience is described as having : it is lived without being observed by a second act. Reflection is a further mode of experience, in which attention turns to experience itself. Neither lived presence nor reflection amounts to complete or infallible knowledge of what is experienced.
  8. The of experience is its being lived from a perspective; it is not a thought or utterance of “I.” Seeing a road, feeling the body lean, anticipating a bend, and thinking “I am on my way” may all occur together. The “I”-thought is one appearance among these. It neither creates the perspective nor shows that a exists apart from experience. This method leaves that question open.

Depth, distance, nearness, and difference are immanent as experienced. Their appearance alone does not settle how independently existing things are related.
  2. includes experienced succession and duration, remembering as remembering, and anticipation as anticipation. Apparent recurrence is admitted without being treated as a necessary law. That a recollection occurs does not by itself show its . This does not imply that historical knowledge is impossible, only that its claims need warrant beyond the fact of remembering.
  3. Description says what appears and how it appears. Explanation adds grounds, causes, mechanisms, or conditions, and these need further argument or evidence. Experiential Immanence neither supplies such explanations nor rules them out. Its own concepts and wording are also interpretations within experience, and are not exempt from its method.
  4. In practice, the method separates what is experienced from what is also endorsed as true or real. It can be used against : treating an anticipated future, an inferred self, or an interpretation as already settled. Pain and distress are admitted in full; description does not understate them. Suspending assent may bring relief; the system does not promise it.
