Experiential Immanence

1Start from what appears

The method starts with whatever appears. This is called , and it includes all of these:

  • seeing
  • hearing
  • touching
  • feeling
  • remembering
  • imagining
  • expecting
  • thinking
  • judging
  • estimating
  • doubting

Every kind counts, even a doubt, a daydream or a mistake. This is called . Some things seem more convincing than others: what you see may feel solid, a memory may feel unsure, and a daydream can be changed at will. Differences like these can make an estimate stronger or weaker (section 3).

What appears often already has a meaning: you see a cup, not just patches of colour. This is called . The method does not assume a layer of raw patches underneath that is later turned into a cup.

The word means the same as appearing. It does not assume a container that holds experiences, or a separate someone who has them.

Starting here is a choice of where to begin. It is not a claim that experience is all there is, or that it is certain.

A description says what appears and how it appears. The same sentence can be a description or a claim: “there seems to be a cup” can simply report how things look, or it can be held as true. The difference is in how it is used, not in its words. Descriptions can still be wrong, because they leave things out and depend on interpretation.

2Leave every claim open

The central practice is , or suspension: a claim is neither accepted as true nor rejected as false. This applies to every claim, including every claim on this page.

Holding a claim as true or known is called . Suspension withholds it, including the careful kind: “this is true, though I might be wrong”. Rejecting a claim means holding its opposite as true, so that is withheld too.

Suspension does not mean feeling nothing. A sense of conviction can stay. It is one more thing that appears.

3Act on estimates

Leaving a claim open does not stop you from acting. You can act on an : a rating of how well what appears supports a claim, from weak to strong. An estimate never reaches certainty. The claim is held as better or worse supported, not as true or false. The estimate itself is also only an estimate.

Acting on an estimate without holding the claim as true is called . From the outside it can look exactly like acting on a belief.

Three ways to hold the claim “the road continues after the bend”

Held as true

“It continues.” Or, carefully: “It continues, though I could be wrong.”

Withheld here

Left open

“Does it continue?” Held neither as true nor as false.

Always

Estimated

“It probably continues.” An estimate from the road seen so far.

Used for acting

If the road ends after the bend

Held as true: the claim is refuted.

Estimated: the estimate is revised. Nothing was held as true, so nothing is refuted.

This is the difference from fallibilism, which holds claims as true while allowing that they might be false.

Anything that appears can make an estimate stronger or weaker: what you see, what you remember, what you work out. You can check your memory against its past successes, but those successes are remembered too, so the check is not independent.

You do not have to act against your estimates, or keep putting your doubts into words.

  • Claims go beyond what appears. Each one turns into a question, and its dashed line leaves the frame: the claim is left open.

4Which big questions get an estimate

Does anything exist beyond what appears?

No estimate is formed for the question as a whole. Smaller claims can still be estimated, such as that the road continues after the bend.

Does the back of the cup exist?

It can be estimated. The front you see supports the estimate that there is a back.

Did the past happen as you remember it?

It can be estimated. A memory can support an estimate about the past.

Will the future be like the past?

It can be estimated. Repeated patterns support an estimate, but they do not make the future certain.

Do other people have experiences?

It can be estimated from how they look, sound and move. On that estimate, they can be treated as people with experiences of their own.

Is there a self that exists in its own right?

No estimate is formed, either way.

Can anything be known?

No estimate is formed. The method does not say that nothing can be known.

Is this method right?

Its own claims are only estimates. It does not claim to be the only right method.

5How things appear

The method also describes how things appear.

In different ways

Something can appear as seen, remembered, imagined or expected. This is its .

About something

A memory of a road is about the road. This is called . Not everything that appears has to be about something: a sudden noise can just be a noise.

With more than is shown

A cup from above. The triangle marks the viewpoint.

You see the near side of a cup. Its far side, its weight when you lift it, and the warmth of the tea are implied, not shown. Together they are the cup’s .

Together and apart

Things appear together: near or far, joined or separate. This is called the . The word does not assume a container that holds everything, or that everything fits into one whole. Gaps and fragments count too.

Without being watched

When you look at the road, there need not be a second act of watching yourself look. Experience described this way is called .

Turning your attention onto experience is called . Attention can change what it looks at: noticing your breathing can change your breathing. So reflection can get things wrong too.

From a perspective

Things often appear “from here”, or as “mine”. This is a . A sense of your body, a sense that something is yours, and the thought “I” are different things, and they need not come together. The thought “I” does not show whether there is a apart from experience.

In time

Things appear as before and after, lasting or changing. The bend appears as coming up; the road behind appears as just passed.

6Reasons and evidence

Arguments, evidence and explanations can all be used. An argument gives reasons, evidence makes a claim stronger or weaker, and an explanation says what causes something. Being something that appears does not make an argument good or bad.

More thinking can change an estimate without ending suspension. The method’s own ideas can be criticised and revised.

7What it is for

The method is practised for one change: something can still feel convincing while being held only as an estimate.

It invites a closer look at how

  • an expectation turns into a settled future,
  • an interpretation turns into a fact,
  • a judgement of good or bad turns into a final verdict,
  • a sense of self turns into a thing that exists.

The change may not happen, and whether it happens is not a test of the method. Pain and distress need not go away. The method promises no relief, and it is not a complete guide to how to live.
